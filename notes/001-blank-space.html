Blank Space.

November 2014 · Oheka Castle, Long Island
The call

I got a call on a Tuesday. A coordinator I'd worked with once. She needed a PA for Thursday. Two days.

I was 26. Steph and I had maybe thirty weddings under our belt. I was still cutting highlights in my basement in Jersey, still figuring out half the keyboard shortcuts in Media Composer.

She said Taylor Swift. She said music video. She said Long Island. I said yes before she finished.

I didn't tell anyone except Steph. Not because it was confidential. Because I didn't believe it was real. Stuff like this doesn't happen to wedding guys in Morristown.

The estate

Oheka Castle. Second-largest private residence ever built in the US. 127,000 square feet of French chateau on Long Island, built in 1919 for a financier whose name nobody on set could spell.

I pulled up and thought I had the wrong address. You don't drive to a music video. You drive to a parking lot. But I'm rolling through formal gardens, past stone lions, watching PAs jog across gravel paths that probably cost more than my car.

I got my pink wristband, found a corner, and opened my laptop. Joseph Kahn was directing. The man doesn't raise his voice. Doesn't need to. Everyone just knows where to be. Sean O'Pry was by craft services looking like someone who'd never taken a bad photo in his life.

Avid · Between setups
Chandelier · Before

On the call sheet I was listed as PA – PER JK. Production assistant, per Joseph Kahn. My phone number one row above the craft service guy. That's the job. You show up, you do whatever needs doing, and you keep your eyes open.

And then Taylor walked on set and the energy didn't shift. It organized. Every light, every crystal on every chandelier, every frame had somewhere to be. Kahn shot the whole thing in about two days. Two days to make the video that would break VEVO's 24-hour record and become the fastest to a billion views.

Between setups I walked those hallways alone. Marble floors. Gold molding. Paintings hanging crooked from the destruction scenes they'd just shot. A golf club through a classic car. Designer clothes cut to ribbons. Red paint thrown across cream canvas.

The cut

Here's the part nobody ever talks about.

I went home that night and opened a wedding timeline. Same laptop. Same Avid session. One window had chandeliers shattering in slow motion at a Long Island castle. The other had a first dance at a banquet hall in Hoboken.

And you know what? The Hoboken wedding was beautiful too.

That's the thing about this work. The timeline doesn't care who you are. Doesn't care if you're Taylor Swift or a bride's cousin's friend. The job is the same. Find the moment. Cut to it. Make it mean something.

That week taught me more than any tutorial ever did. Not because the production was bigger. Because I realized the thing I was already doing, sitting alone at 4 AM, moving clips around, trying to make strangers cry at their own wedding, that was the same muscle. Same eye. Same gut feeling when a cut lands right.

I was a PA on a Taylor Swift set. But I was also the guy cutting a Hoboken wedding at midnight. Both of those are me. They always have been.

The timeline doesn't care who you are. Taylor Swift or a bride in Hoboken. Find the moment. Cut to it.
